SNAP changes take effect October 1: small COLA bump, big new costs for states

A small cost-of-living bump for recipients, but states face a big new cost burden — and analysts warn future benefits could be at risk.
Unspun · Listen: 3:40

Two changes to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program take effect October 1, according to USA Today reporting. About 37 million Americans receive SNAP benefits.

Most recipients will see a small cost-of-living increase. In the 48 contiguous states plus the District of Columbia, maximum monthly benefits rise $8 for single recipients, $23 for a family of three, $29 for a family of four, and $34 for a family of five. Amounts differ in Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, and the U.S. Virgin Islands.

At the same time, under the tax-and-spending law passed last year, states' share of SNAP administrative costs jumps from 50 percent to 75 percent starting October 1. Beginning October 1, 2027, most states would have to pay a portion of benefit costs for the first time, based on each state's error rate in determining eligibility and benefit amounts. The Senate Agriculture Committee's Farm Bill, which passed committee in September, proposes delaying that cost-sharing by one year to October 1, 2028, while increasing penalties for states with the highest error rates.

The National Association of Counties estimates counties face up to $850 million in added annual administrative obligations. Farm Aid estimates individual states could owe between $15 million and $1.5 billion each. USDA data through March show SNAP has lost more than 4.7 million participants nationwide since July 2025, with Arizona losing nearly half its enrollees. In Arizona, about 843,000 people used a food pantry in April, up about 8 percent from a year earlier and surpassing SNAP enrollment, according to the Arizona Food Bank Network.

The Urban Institute estimates 832,000 students could lose free school meals or be required to file separate applications if their parents lose SNAP. Analysts describe future benefit cuts as a scenario dependent on state decisions and the Farm Bill, not a certainty. Stacy Dean of George Washington University's Global Food Institute called the policy of demanding more from states while the federal government cuts back 'cynical,' and No Kid Hungry's Carolyn Vega said some state agencies have raised the possibility of opting out of SNAP entirely.

Sources: USA Today, U.S. Department of Agriculture, National Association of Counties, Farm Aid, Urban Institute, Arizona Food Bank Network
