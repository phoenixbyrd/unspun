Supreme Court allows expanded use of SAVE citizenship database for voter checks

The 6-3 emergency order permits individualized inquiries but warns against mass purges 38 days before the midterms.
Unspun · Listen: 6:04

The Supreme Court ruled Friday, in an unsigned 6-3 emergency order, that the Department of Homeland Security may resume using a revamped federal database for voter citizenship checks, lifting a June ruling by D.C. district judge Sparkle L. Sooknanan. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson dissented.

The database, SAVE (Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements), dates to 1986 and was built to verify immigration status for public benefits. After the president's March 2025 executive order on voting, DHS linked SAVE to Social Security Administration data and enabled bulk searches by name, birthday, and Social Security number. Judge Sooknanan found in a 75-page opinion that officials had "haphazardly combined and repurposed the private information of millions of Americans, including citizenship data that they knew to be unreliable."

The majority permitted "individualized" inquiries, warning it is too late in the 2026 cycle for mass voter-roll purges. The order is interim and does not resolve the underlying lawsuit. The majority said challengers' claims "likely lack merit," citing a 1996 immigration law obligating DHS to answer states' citizenship questions. The dissent reads that duty as not authorizing a bulk-search system built from benefits-verification infrastructure.

The government told the Court it has checked more than 65 million registrations in 26 states and flagged 28,635 people as possible noncitizens. Those are flags, not confirmed noncitizen voters; no public data breaks down confirmations versus errors. The system's error rate has not been publicly disclosed. The court record includes a plaintiff whose registration was revoked without her knowledge and two others who had to prove citizenship.

States' use is voluntary, and the National Voter Registration Act bars most systematic removals within 90 days of a federal election. The midterms are November 3. Justice Jackson wrote that "the harm caused by burdening or disenfranchising even a few lawful voters outweighs the nonexistent harm that the Government experiences when it is prevented from taking an action that it likely lacks the authority to take."

Sources: Supreme Court order, D.C. district court opinion, Reuters, League of Women Voters
