Justice Alito steps aside from Boulder climate case; 4-4 split possible

No reason given for the recusal ahead of the Oct. 5 argument. A tie would let Boulder's lawsuit proceed without setting national precedent.
Unspun · Listen: 5:38

The Supreme Court announced Monday that Justice Samuel Alito will not take part in next week's landmark climate case, opening the possibility of a four-to-four split. He gave no reason, according to USA Today's reporting.

The case: the city of Boulder and surrounding county sued in 2018, alleging ExxonMobil and Suncor violated state law by misleading the public about the environmental effects of fossil fuels and should help cover climate-related community costs. The companies deny the allegations and argue local governments cannot use state law for what they call a global issue. The appeal is argued October 5, the first case of the court's new term.

Environmental groups had urged recusal because Alito owns stock in ConocoPhillips and Phillips 66, both targeted in similar suits. Alexandra Nagy of Consumer Watchdog said that as a shareholder he had been warned climate-deception suits pose financial risks to his investments. The stock-holdings explanation is an inference from critics, not a confirmed fact.

The history adds texture. Alito recused from the 2023 decision on whether the court should intervene at an earlier stage of the Colorado litigation, and did not participate in the court's 2025 rejection of a similar Hawaii appeal. In May, a court spokeswoman told NBC News that legal counsel had advised recusal was not required because “he does not have a financial interest in any party.” Four months later, he is recusing from the merits argument, with no reason given.

The arithmetic: with a 6-3 bench, one recusal opens a possible 4-4 split. If that happens, the oil companies' appeal fails and Boulder's lawsuit proceeds in the lower courts — but no national precedent is set, leaving dozens of similar suits without a Supreme Court answer. USA Today characterized the stakes as billions of dollars potentially at issue.

The underlying legal question is whether cities and counties can use state law to seek climate-damage costs from oil companies. If municipalities can recover adaptation costs from energy companies, the funding model for climate resilience changes nationwide; if they cannot, taxpayers fund sea walls, stormwater systems, and wildfire response. The direction of the outcome is unresolved.

Sources: USA Today, NBC News, U.S. Supreme Court, Consumer Watchdog
