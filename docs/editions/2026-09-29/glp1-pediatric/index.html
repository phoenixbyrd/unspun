Study: 1 in 6 children on GLP-1 drugs developed a nutritional deficiency

Vitamin D deficiency was most common at 12.4%. Only 5% got nutritional counseling within 30 days. Researchers urge monitoring from day one.
Unspun · Listen: 5:28

A new study finds that nearly one in six children prescribed GLP-1 weight-loss drugs developed a nutritional deficiency within a year, while almost none received nutritional counseling.

The study by Kirk W. Kerr and colleagues — “Nutritional Deficiencies, Complications, and Nutrition Therapy/Counseling in Pediatric Patients Using GLP-1 Receptor Agonists” — was published in the journal Childhood Obesity in 2026 and surfaced in a Medical Dialogues bulletin September 28.

Researchers analyzed national administrative claims data from 2017 through 2022, a database covering more than 100 million patients. They identified 2,031 children aged 10 to 17 who had received GLP-1 medications, had continuous health coverage, and had no previous nutritional-deficiency diagnosis. The continuous-coverage requirement means these are children whose medical records could actually be tracked.

Within one year of starting treatment, 16.8 percent were diagnosed with a nutritional deficiency. Vitamin D deficiency was most common at 12.4 percent. GLP-1 medications reduce appetite and food intake, which may make it harder for some children to obtain essential nutrients — and adolescence is a period of rapid growth when vitamin D, iron, and calcium are particularly important for bone development.

The counseling gap is stark: only 5 percent of the children received nutritional counseling within 30 days of starting treatment; fewer than 25 percent received it within six months. The researchers recommend nutritional monitoring begin when GLP-1 treatment starts, rather than after a deficiency develops.

The limits are stated by the researchers themselves. A retrospective claims-data study finds association, not causation — it cannot prove the medication caused the deficiencies, and children prescribed these drugs may differ from peers in ways the data cannot capture. Further research is needed, in the researchers' words, on how treatment affects nutrition during childhood and on monitoring and prevention approaches. With adolescent GLP-1 use growing, the study frames a practical care gap: a one-in-six deficiency rate within a year, vitamin D the most common gap, and counseling largely absent.

Sources: Childhood Obesity (journal), Medical Dialogues, Kirk W. Kerr
