Supreme Court lets states use revamped voter-citizenship database

An unsigned Friday stay paused a ruling that found privacy violations and wrongful-removal risk. The 90-day purge bar limits immediate midterm impact.
Unspun · Listen: 5:50

Five weeks before the November 3 midterms, the Supreme Court has let states use a revamped federal voter-citizenship database, while the fight over whether it is lawful continues.

On Friday, September 25, the Supreme Court issued an unsigned emergency stay pausing a June 22 ruling by U.S. District Judge Sparkle L. Sooknanan (D.D.C.). She had struck down the administration's revamped Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) program for voter checks, finding it violated privacy rights and risked wrongly removing voters, according to the Associated Press via the National News Desk.

SAVE was built to check immigration status for benefit eligibility. The revamp expanded it for voter-roll citizenship checks, adding Social Security Administration records and bulk-search capacity by executive order. The unsigned order lets states voluntarily use the expanded database while the appeal proceeds. Voluntary is the operative word: no state is required to use it. The court did not rule on whether the program is lawful; the underlying legality question is undecided and will be litigated past the election.

Justices Ketanji Brown Jackson, Elena Kagan, and Sonia Sotomayor dissented. Jackson warned the database could wrongly flag lawful voters — a concern with a factual anchor. The AP reported that in Travis County, Texas, officials investigated 97 voters flagged by SAVE as apparent noncitizens and found at least 10 percent, likely up to 21 percent, were U.S. citizens. A double-digit false-positive rate on citizenship flags is the difference between a clean roll and a disputed one.

There is a practical limit that matters more than the stay. Federal law bars most states from systematic voter-roll purges within 90 days of a federal election, and the country is inside that window. The AP assesses immediate midterm impact as likely limited; Norm Eisen of Brookings concurred, pointing to the decision's own acknowledgment of the 90-day window. Individual removals, as opposed to systematic purges, can still proceed in many states.

A secondary outlet reported September 28 that a Florida judge also ordered database access for several states, disagreeing with the June ruling, citing PBS and the AP. That could not be verified against a primary source and remains developing. The verified bottom line: the database is usable by states as of Friday under an unsigned stay with three dissents; the June ruling's findings are paused, not overturned; and the Travis County check remains the hard evidence on accuracy — at least one in ten flagged voters was a citizen.

Sources: Associated Press, National News Desk, U.S. Supreme Court, U.S. District Judge Sparkle L. Sooknanan, Brookings Institution, Travis County officials
