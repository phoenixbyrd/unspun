Gulf oil exports back to 72% of prewar levels; Iranian rial hits record low

Kpler data shows Hormuz flows at their wartime highest even as ship traffic lags. Iran's currency has collapsed to 2.45 million rials per dollar.
Unspun · Listen: 3:53

Seven months into the U.S.-Iran war, the energy corridor through the Strait of Hormuz is flowing again, and Iran's currency is in free fall. Monday brought hard numbers on both, from maritime tracker Kpler as reported by the New York Post.

Crude exports from the Persian Gulf averaged more than 13 million barrels a day in September — 72 percent of prewar levels. Oil flow through the Strait of Hormuz itself averaged 7.3 million barrels a day in September, the highest during the war, with about 17 ships a day transiting — still less than half of prewar ship traffic.

Energy Secretary Chris Wright said on Fox News on Sunday, September 27, that one day in the previous week saw over 20 million barrels flow out of the Strait, more than pre-conflict levels. That is a named official's statement, not independently verified flow data. Additional Gulf flows moved through ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman and the UAE's Fujairah port and pipeline, which bypasses the Strait entirely.

Unnamed U.S. officials told the Post that rising oil flow increases American negotiating leverage. The hard numbers are Kpler's, an independent tracker, but readers should note the framing comes from U.S. officials, and the Post is a partisan outlet.

On the other side of the ledger, the Iranian rial fell to a record low of 2.45 million per U.S. dollar, versus 42,000 a year ago. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent wrote on X: “Operation Economic Outcast has caused the rial to hit record lows. We will continue to degrade the Iranian regime's ability to fund terrorism and develop a nuclear weapon.”

The connection to American households: Hormuz oil flows are a direct driver of U.S. gas prices, and the recovering flows are the supply side of the $4.48 national gasoline average. The picture is asymmetrical — Gulf oil moving again under U.S. military protection at nearly three-quarters of prewar levels, while the blockade has collapsed Iran's currency by a factor of roughly 58 against the dollar in a year.

Sources: Kpler, New York Post, Energy Secretary Chris Wright, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Bloomberg, Military.com
