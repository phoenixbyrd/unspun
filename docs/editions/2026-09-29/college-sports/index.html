Senate passes college sports overhaul 77-22, sending it to the House

Federal NIL rights, revenue sharing, a 5% agent-fee cap, and 10-year scholarship guarantees for 500,000-plus athletes — if the House acts.
Unspun · Listen: 6:00

The U.S. Senate on Monday passed the Protect College Sports Act 77-22, the first federal overhaul of college athlete compensation rules, sending it to the House. The Senate invoked cloture September 24 by 74-25. The official release came from the Senate Commerce Committee, with Reuters reporting the vote.

The bill was written by Sens. Maria Cantwell (D-Wash.) and Ted Cruz (R-Texas) and introduced in June. It creates a federal right to name, image, and likeness compensation, replacing the patchwork of state laws, school policies, and booster-funded collectives that has governed NIL since 2021.

Schools must share a percentage of media rights, ticket sales, and sponsorships with athletes — a revenue-sharing requirement. Agent fees are capped at 5 percent. Scholarships are guaranteed for 10 years after eligibility ends. Division I schools must cover out-of-pocket medical costs for sports-related injuries and illness for five years after eligibility. Schools cannot pull scholarships over injury or poor performance. Athletes may transfer once with immediate eligibility. Football coaches are barred from midseason moves. Schools must maintain a minimum number of women's and Olympic sports.

Cruz, the bill's Republican architect, said on the floor: “The most important part of the bill is bringing order to chaos,” as reported by USA Today. Sen. Cory Booker (D-N.J.) opposed it: “I regret this day... a bill that's going to hurt so many,” citing pressure from civil-rights and labor leaders and concern the bill disempowers athletes by locking in terms they did not negotiate.

The athlete-protection provisions reach into daily life: the 10-year scholarship guarantee, five years of injury medical coverage, and the ban on pulling scholarships over injury or poor performance — aimed at programs quietly pushing out hurt or underperforming players to free roster spots. The women's and Olympic sports minimum tries to prevent football and basketball money from cannibalizing non-revenue programs.

The bill now goes to the House, whose next scheduled legislative session begins November 9, six days after the midterms. Whether the House acts at all is unresolved, especially with a possible post-election power shift. NBC Sports cited an unnamed SI.com source saying there is “0.0 percent chance” the House passes it before the midterms — anonymous, and to be treated as speculation. Cruz's floor remarks, via the Commerce release, said the bill affects more than half a million college athletes.

Sources: U.S. Senate Commerce Committee, Reuters, USA Today, Sen. Ted Cruz, Sen. Cory Booker
