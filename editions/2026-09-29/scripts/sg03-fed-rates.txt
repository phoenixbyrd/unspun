Alex: Markets are now betting heavily that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates again next month. Here are the numbers behind that bet, and what they mean for borrowing costs.

Jordan: The C M E Group's FedWatch tool pegs the odds of an October rate hike at about seventy percent, with December hike odds at roughly ninety-two percent, according to Barron's reporting on Monday. Those are market-priced probabilities, not a Federal Reserve decision. The Fed has not announced anything.

Alex: The backdrop: on September sixteenth, the Federal Open Market Committee voted twelve to zero to raise the federal funds target to a range of three point seven five to four percent. That was the first hike since July of twenty twenty-three. The dot plot median implies rates reach four point one percent by the end of twenty twenty-six, according to reporting on the Fed's Summary of Economic Projections.

Jordan: What moved the odds: energy prices. With oil settling above one hundred five dollars a barrel on Monday, economists warn that inflation could accelerate. The question for the Fed's October meeting is whether the September inflation data comes in hot enough to force another move.

Alex: Citi Research weighed in Monday with its outlook. Economists Andrew Hollenhorst and Veronica Clark project September nonfarm payrolls of about eighty-five thousand, with the unemployment rate edging from four point one to four point two percent. They say the September core consumer price index print is the decisive variable for October. Only negative payrolls, or unemployment jumping to four point three percent, would force markets to reassess, according to the report.

Jordan: The September jobs report is due Friday, October second. The inflation data follows in the weeks after. So the seventy percent figure is markets pricing in expectations, not a settled outcome. If the data surprises either way, those odds will move.

Alex: Meanwhile, borrowing costs are already at striking levels. The thirty-year fixed mortgage rate is averaging more than seven percent, the highest since January of twenty twenty-five, according to the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey. That is the rate that sets monthly payments for homebuyers and for anyone refinancing.

Jordan: EY-Parthenon chief economist Gregory Daco, in his September twenty-eighth newsletter, put it bluntly: interest rate changes cannot produce energy, resolve supply chain pressures, expand the labor force, or increase semiconductor capacity. In other words, rate hikes cannot fix the supply-side forces pushing prices up. Daco expects an additional quarter-point hike in December. He projects full-year twenty twenty-six inflation at two point nine percent, and notes food prices are up two point seven percent year over year as of August twenty twenty-six.

Alex: Fed Governor Lisa Cook addressed the question Monday, saying the labor market appears to be well positioned to handle an increase in rates. That is the Fed's internal read: the job market can absorb more tightening.

Jordan: Connect the threads. Higher rates mean higher costs on mortgages, car loans, credit cards, and home equity lines of credit. Those hit household budgets directly. Savings account yields tend to rise more slowly, so the burden falls harder on borrowers than the benefit rises for savers.

Alex: The numbers in sequence: rates went up on September sixteenth to three point seven five to four percent. Markets now give October another hike a seventy percent chance, December a ninety-two percent chance. Mortgages are above seven percent. The September jobs report lands Friday, and the inflation prints after that will decide whether the market's bet pays off.

Jordan: One more point of calibration: the hike odds are derived from futures contracts. They represent the collective wager of traders, not inside information. In past cycles, these odds have swung sharply on a single data release. With energy prices this volatile, the October decision is genuinely unresolved.

Alex: So the watch items: Friday's jobs report, where Citi expects about eighty-five thousand new payrolls and unemployment at four point two percent. Then the September core C P I. If inflation runs hot, the Fed has room in its own projection to go to four point one percent by year's end. If the data cools, markets will reprice just as fast.
