Alex: Alabama has reached a settlement with TikTok over claims the platform endangered children.

Jordan: The state announced Friday, September twenty-fifth, that it had settled with TikTok and its parent company ByteDance, days before a trial scheduled to begin Monday, September twenty-eighth, in Montgomery state court. Alabama's attorney general, Steve Marshall, sued the company last year. He called the agreement quote a great day for Alabama parents, end quote.

Alex: What does TikTok have to do?

Jordan: Impose new usage limits for teenagers in Alabama: a two-hour daily time limit, pauses after fifteen minutes of use, stricter age checks, restricted access overnight, and suspended notifications during school hours. TikTok will also ban beauty filters for teenagers and offer young users a nonpersonalized content feed. The overnight block runs midnight to six a m, with a conditional expansion to ten p m to seven a m if other platforms make the same commitment. Some measures phase in over up to a year.

Alex: And the money?

Jordan: TikTok pays Alabama at least one hundred million dollars within forty-five days, growing to as much as three hundred million if forty other state attorneys general sign similar agreements within a specified timeframe. The exact timeframe was not specified in the reporting reviewed. It is TikTok's first settlement with an American state.

Alex: How widespread is this litigation?

Jordan: At least twenty-seven other states and Washington, D C, have brought similar lawsuits. The Alabama settlement mirrors safety provisions Meta agreed to in August, when it settled a sweeping state lawsuit over Instagram and Facebook for eighteen billion dollars.

Alex: What did the settlement decide about the underlying claims?

Jordan: Nothing. The claims were settled, not adjudicated. No court ruled on whether TikTok endangered children or misled consumers. A settlement ends the case without a finding of fact.

Alex: Go back to how this case started.

Jordan: Alabama sued in April twenty twenty-five, focusing on the state's Deceptive Trade Practices Act. State investigators posing as teenagers reported seeing content about suicide, self-harm, eating disorders, and alcohol even with Restricted Mode active.

Alex: The trial would have been unusual.

Jordan: It had been expected to last two to three weeks and to provide a rare look inside TikTok. Reuters noted the company had settled prior cases selected for trial and sought to keep details of its operations from public view.

Alex: How did the settlement come together?

Jordan: It was finalized shortly before the jury trial was to begin, following mediation in which the settlement represented Alabama's final offer. TikTok and ByteDance agreed to pay Alabama one hundred million dollars within forty-five days.

Alex: ByteDance is part of the deal as well.

Jordan: Yes. The B B C reported ByteDance was included because it retains a minority stake in TikTok's U S business following a corporate restructuring earlier this year.

Alex: The total could reach three hundred million. How does that work?

Jordan: The additional payment, reported at up to one hundred eighty-three point eight million dollars beyond the base one hundred million, depends on enough other states reaching qualifying settlements with TikTok within a specific timeframe. The state said the money would go toward restitution and remediation.

Alex: What about enforcement of the safety terms?

Jordan: If TikTok fails to comply, it faces additional penalties. The pauses take effect at fifteen, sixty, and ninety minutes of uninterrupted use. TikTok must also notify parents about suspicious interactions between teens and adults, and limit adult users' ability to discover children's accounts.

Alex: This follows another settlement this summer.

Jordan: In August, TikTok reached a four hundred million dollar settlement with the Department of Justice over allegations the platform violated child privacy laws, according to TechCrunch. The Alabama deal is separate and modeled on the Meta agreement from August.
