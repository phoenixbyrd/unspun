Jordan: An important qualifier first. The final regulatory text has not been released. Saturday's announcement was a social media post, not a published rule. It still has to go through formal regulatory procedures.

Alex: What would the change do?

Jordan: A December twenty twenty-five proposal from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration would cut the fleetwide fuel-economy target from about fifty point four miles per gallon by twenty thirty-one down to about thirty-four point five miles per gallon for cars and light trucks.

Alex: Give the history of these standards.

Jordan: CAFE, Corporate Average Fuel Economy, dates to nineteen seventy-five, created after the Arab oil embargo. Instead of telling automakers what cars to build, it sets a fleetwide average they have to hit and lets them decide how to get there. The Biden administration's version pushed the target to about fifty point four miles per gallon by twenty thirty-one, which effectively required a large share of electric vehicle sales, because that average cannot be hit on gasoline engines alone.

Alex: And the rollback target?

Jordan: About thirty-four point five miles per gallon by twenty thirty-one, under the December proposal. That is roughly where the industry already is, which is why automakers support it. It removes the regulatory pressure to electrify.

Alex: What is the administration's case?

Jordan: Its projection: save Americans one hundred nine billion dollars over five years and cut the average new-vehicle price by nearly one thousand dollars compared to the Biden standards. The president wrote it will mean quote lower prices, saving families thousands on a new, beautiful, and safe car, end quote. Congress has already zeroed out the fines for automakers that miss the mileage standards. Automakers including G M, Ford, and Stellantis welcomed the change and announced billions in new American manufacturing investment.

Alex: And the other side of the ledger?

Jordan: The traffic safety administration's own modeling projects up to one hundred billion more gallons of fuel burned through twenty fifty under the rollback. Environmental groups cite E P A analysis that eliminating the standards raises lifetime fuel costs for drivers.

Alex: Which cost claim is right?

Jordan: Both sides' numbers are projections with disputed assumptions. The direction of each effect is clear. Less stringent standards mean cheaper sticker prices and thirstier cars. The magnitudes, how much cheaper and how much thirstier, are not settled. The precise dollar figures are model outputs, not measured facts.

Alex: What happens Monday?

Jordan: Transportation Secretary Duffy is expected to formally announce the final rule. Until the text is published, the exact targets, the timeline, and whether the December proposal changed are unknown. These rules also face a standard legal gauntlet: publication, a window for legal challenges, and likely lawsuits from states and environmental groups. The Biden standards were litigated for years.

The Transportation Department's own estimates say the new standards would cut the cost of new vehicles but increase fuel consumption and carbon dioxide emissions for decades. The department estimated the proposal would cut average new-vehicle costs by nine hundred thirty dollars each, but increase fuel consumption by around one hundred billion gallons through twenty fifty, fuel spending by one hundred eighty-five billion dollars, and carbon dioxide emissions by about five percent.

Jordan: Reuters noted the push comes as American drivers face sharply higher fuel prices since the start of the conflict with Iran at the end of February. Duffy said the rollback would cut at least one thousand dollars from the price of vehicles bought in America.

Alex: Walk through the mechanics of the December proposal.

Jordan: The December proposal would retroactively revise down the twenty twenty-two model year fuel economy standard, then raise it between a quarter of one percent and half of one percent annually through twenty thirty-one. By contrast, the Biden administration had increased required fuel efficiency for cars by eight percent annually for model years twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five, ten percent for twenty twenty-six, and two percent annually from twenty twenty-seven to twenty thirty-one.

Jordan: The administration has also eased tailpipe emissions rules, ended fines for automakers that miss targets, and ended consumer credits of up to seven thousand five hundred dollars for electric vehicle purchases.
