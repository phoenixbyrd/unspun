Alex: Federal health officials have linked a multistate E. coli outbreak to a raw-milk cheese.

Jordan: The C D C and F D A announced September twenty-fifth that an outbreak of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O twenty-six colon H eleven has been linked to Graziers grass-fed raw-milk cheese, made by Sierra Nevada Cheese Company of Willows, California. Thirteen people in nine states have fallen ill: three each in California and Nevada, and one each in Colorado, Georgia, Kentucky, Michigan, Oregon, Tennessee, and Utah. Eight were hospitalized and three developed hemolytic uremic syndrome, a type of kidney failure. No deaths were reported.

Alex: Who is getting sick?

Jordan: More than half of the patients are children age five or younger. Ages range from one to fifty-eight, with a median age of five. All nine people interviewed about foods eaten before their illness reported eating the cheese. Illness onsets ran from July seventh to August twenty-sixth.

Alex: What is being recalled?

Jordan: All four Graziers raw-milk varieties: medium cheddar, sharp cheddar, jalapeno jack, and Monterey jack. They were recalled September twenty-fourth after the F D A contacted the company. The cheese was sold nationwide in stores and online, and it has a long shelf life, so the F D A advises checking refrigerators and freezers and discarding any unidentifiable raw-milk cheese.

Alex: What is still unknown?

Jordan: The source of the contamination and whether additional products are affected remain under investigation. Health officials caution the true case count may be higher, since many people recover without being tested. This is the fourth raw-milk cheese outbreak in about two and a half years, with forty-three sick total according to the Marler Blog. No regulatory change has been announced.

Alex: How was the cheese sold?

Jordan: The recalled cheeses were sold in eight-ounce and sixteen-ounce retail packages, and to restaurants, food-service establishments, and wholesalers in five-pound blocks. They were distributed nationwide and sold online.

Alex: What does the F D A advise?

Jordan: The agency says do not eat, sell, or serve the recalled Sierra Nevada Graziers grass-fed raw milk cheeses. It also advises anyone who handled the recalled cheese to clean and sanitize surfaces and containers it touched, to reduce the risk of cross-contamination.

Alex: What are the symptoms to watch for?

Jordan: E. coli infections can cause severe stomach cramps, diarrhea, fever, nausea, and vomiting. Diarrhea can become bloody in more serious infections. Symptoms can begin several days after exposure and, in some cases, as late as nine days afterward, the F D A says. Anyone who ate the recalled cheese and develops symptoms should contact a health care provider and mention the possible E. coli exposure.

Alex: Why might the count grow?

Jordan: Health officials caution the true case count may be higher, since many people recover without being tested. According to the C D C, for every confirmed patient in an E. coli outbreak, twenty-six go undetected. Illness onsets ran from July seventh to August twenty-sixth.

Alex: How severe were the illnesses?

Jordan: Three patients developed hemolytic uremic syndrome. Food Safety News describes H U S as a type of kidney failure that can also cause other serious problems, including brain damage and death. No deaths were reported in this outbreak.

Alex: One more note on detection.

Jordan: Food contaminated with E. coli may not look, smell, or taste spoiled. Appearance and taste cannot reliably indicate whether a product is safe, which is why officials say to discard any raw-milk cheese that cannot be identified.
